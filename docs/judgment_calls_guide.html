Judgment calls in data processing for machine learning

Every decision between a sensor and a model is a choice someone made. Most go unrecorded, and the ones that go unrecorded are the ones that move the answer. This is a list of where they hide, what to look at to find them, and what a bad answer looks like.

The organizing idea, which comes out of the veridical data science framing, is that a result is a claim resting on a chain: raw signal, then preprocessing, then features, then labels, then sampling unit, then model, then evaluation, then the conclusion. Weakness anywhere upstream caps the credibility of everything downstream, so you audit in order. A fancier model cannot repair a broken label.

What makes these judgment calls rather than errors is that a competent analyst could defensibly answer each one differently. That is exactly why they need recording. Yu and Barter's central empirical finding is worth holding onto: the uncertainty introduced by reasonable variation in cleaning choices is often comparable in magnitude to the uncertainty from resampling the data. Conventional confidence intervals capture only the second.

01Provenance

Before anything else, ask how many transformations sit between the sensor and the table you were handed, and whether any of them were lossy and irreversible.

What to look at

Count distinct values per column against what the instrument's resolution could physically produce. Compare the file you have against the original deposit's file list. Ask what the data looked like one step before it reached you.

Signature of a problem

A continuous physiological channel with far fewer distinct values than rows. If heart rate shows a few thousand unique values across millions of rows, something forward-filled it onto a common grid, and the within-window variance you were about to compute is an artifact of the fill.

Channels that exist in the source deposit and not in your file. Their absence is a decision someone made for you.

Worked example. A merged convenience CSV of the nurse dataset forward-filled every channel onto one time grid. That single step dropped blood volume pulse and inter-beat intervals, which are the only sources of heart rate variability, and collapsed heart rate and electrodermal activity to a few unique values per window. The fix was returning to the original deposit and loading each channel at its own native rate.

02The unit of observation

What is a row, and how many genuinely independent things does your dataset contain? These are usually three different numbers and people report the largest.

What to look at

Write down three counts: rows, independent episodes, and independent people. Then compute the ratio between them.

Signature of a problem

A rows-to-independent-units ratio above roughly a hundred. Every standard error you compute from the row count is then wrong by more than an order of magnitude, and any split that does not respect the independent unit leaks.

Overlapping windows. A 50 percent hop means adjacent rows share half their raw samples, so they are near-copies rather than observations.

Worked example. Roughly 4,869 windows, drawn from 178 events, drawn from 14 people. The honest denominator for a claim about generalizing to a new nurse is 14. The honest denominator for a claim about detecting an episode is 178. The window count is a compute statistic and belongs nowhere near a confidence interval.

03Time and alignment

Whenever two sources are joined on time, ask whether both are on the same clock, at the same rate, with the same convention for what a timestamp marks.

What to look at

Print the min and max timestamp of each source side by side. Then count how many joins actually succeed, and compare that against how many should. Read sampling rates from the file itself rather than from documentation.

Signature of a problem

A join that returns suspiciously few rows and no error. This is the worst class of bug because nothing fails. A five-hour offset between a naive local timestamp and a UTC one will place nearly every annotated event outside its recording, and the pipeline will report zero positives rather than raising anything.

Documentation and device specification disagreeing on sampling rate. When they do, the file header is authoritative for your extraction and both documents are irrelevant.

Worked example. Survey timestamps in America/Chicago, sensor timestamps in UTC. The diagnostic is one line: count events whose start falls inside a recording session belonging to the same person. A low count is not a puzzle, it is the signature.

04Missingness

Three separate questions, and people usually answer only the first. How much is missing. Where is it missing. And whether the pattern of missingness carries information about the outcome.

What to look at

A missingness matrix, rows against columns, shaded by presence. Then missingness rate broken down by class. Then missingness rate broken down by any covariate you suspect drives it.

The decisive test: fit a model whose only features are missingness indicators, and see whether it predicts the label. If it does, missingness is a feature, and any model with access to it will use it.

Signature of a problem

Missingness that clusters by person rather than scattering. That means the missing-data mechanism is confounded with the unit you split on.

Missingness correlated with the outcome through a physical mechanism. Sensor quality degrades under motion, motion tracks activity, activity tracks the condition you are trying to detect. Now your missingness indicator is a proxy for the label.

A caution people miss: gradient boosting libraries route missing values natively. A tree can split on missingness whether or not you supply an indicator column. Deciding not to impute does not close this door by itself.

The classical taxonomy is worth knowing but is less useful than the direct test above. Missing completely at random means the missingness is independent of everything. Missing at random means it depends on observed variables. Missing not at random means it depends on the unobserved value itself. Physiological sensor dropout is almost never the first, usually the second, and sometimes the third.

05Exclusions, and their order

Every dropped row is a judgment call. The subtle part is that exclusion rules do not commute, so the order is a second judgment call hiding behind the first.

What to look at

An attrition table. One row per rule, in application order, showing the quantity entering, the quantity removed, and the quantity surviving. Every downstream count should trace back to this table.

Then run it again with two rules swapped and see whether the final count changes.

Signature of a problem

The final count depends on rule order. That means order is a judgment call and must be recorded and perturbed like any other.

An exclusion rule whose threshold was chosen after seeing how it affected results. This is the most common and least visible form of the garden of forking paths, because no model was fitted when the choice was made.

Worked example. Dropping non-wear seconds and then computing session length gives a different session count than computing session length and then dropping non-wear. Both are defensible. Only one is what you did, and the write-up should say which.

06Label construction

Ask who produced the label, under what incentive, at what time resolution, and whether that resolution matches the unit you are predicting.

What to look at

The distribution of label durations. The count of labels per person. And the single most informative plot available: the event-triggered average, where you align every annotated episode at its onset and average the raw signal across a window spanning before and after.

Signature of a problem

A flat event-triggered average. If nothing measurable happens when the annotation says something happened, the label does not mark a physiologically distinguishable state at your resolution, and no modeling repairs that. Better to learn this in week two.

Label durations with an implausible tail. A reported episode lasting five hours is either a genuine sustained state or an annotation left open, and the two demand different handling.

Label counts varying several-fold between people. That means annotation propensity is a person-level variable, which matters enormously if you split on person.

The specific failure worth naming is treating an interval label as per-instant truth. A reported thirty-minute episode is not thirty minutes of continuous state. It may be an eight-minute core surrounded by calm. The principled response is multiple-instance learning, where the interval is a bag labeled "at least one window inside is positive" and the model learns which minutes drove it.

07The comparison group

Sometimes the negative class is observed. Often it is constructed, and the construction is described in one sentence of methods and never varied.

What to look at

Covariate balance between the positive class and the candidate comparison group, before and after whatever construction you apply. Overlaid densities on every variable a model could exploit, especially activity, time of day, and anything person-specific.

Also the achievable size of the comparison group per unit, not in aggregate. An aggregate ratio that looks comfortable can conceal units that cannot supply any comparison at all.

Signature of a problem

Residual imbalance after construction. It means the pool had no mass in the region the positives occupy, so matching silently degraded into filtering.

An aggregate ratio quoted without the per-unit breakdown. Class balance that varies across units also varies your decision threshold across units, which compounds with anything else that already threatens threshold transfer.

The general rule: match, do not filter. Filtering the comparison group toward convenient values creates exactly the confound the comparison was meant to remove. If positives contain motion and you build negatives from resting periods, you have built a motion detector and it will score beautifully.

08Features, and leakage through them

Two questions. Does this feature encode the unit you split on, and does computing it use information the model would not have at deployment.

What to look at

The intraclass correlation of each feature with respect to the split unit. High values mean the feature is largely a fingerprint of the person rather than a measurement of the state.

Then a direct probe: train a classifier to predict the split unit from the feature vector. Whatever accuracy it reaches is the shortcut available to your real model.

Signature of a problem

An intraclass correlation above roughly 0.5 for a feature you were planning to use raw. Absolute skin temperature is the standard offender in wearable work, because everyone runs at their own baseline.

Any normalization statistic computed over the full record. Using a person's whole week to define their baseline uses their future, and it is not reproducible at deployment where no future exists. Causal trailing windows are both leak-free and deployable.

09The split

Ask what is shared between training and test at each candidate level of splitting, then split at the level where the answer is "nothing".

What to look at

List the levels: sample, window, episode, session, day, person. For each, ask what two rows on opposite sides could share. Overlapping windows share raw samples. Two windows from one session share a sensor placement. Two sessions from one person share a physiology.

Signature of a problem

Accuracy that seems too good for the sample size. A random window split on autocorrelated sensor data will produce near-perfect numbers that mean nothing.

A validation set that shares units with the training set. Thresholds and stopping points chosen there are fitted to those units rather than estimated for a new one.

10Balance, and the two ledgers

Keep two things separate that people routinely merge. Prevalence is how rare the positive class is. Separability is how far apart the classes sit. They have different fixes and conflating them means you cannot attribute a gain to the right cause.

What to look at

Report the majority-class baseline next to every accuracy-like number. Under heavy imbalance a model can post a respectable score while a constant predictor posts a better one.

Signature of a problem

Synthetic oversampling applied to autocorrelated time series. Interpolating between two adjacent windows manufactures a near-copy of a real sample. Applied before the split it leaks outright, and applied after it inflates apparent separability without adding information.

A metric that is not estimable in your setting being reported anyway. If your negative class is constructed and possibly contaminated, precision is a statement about your construction rather than about the world, and reporting it as performance is a category error.

Features move separability. Resampling and threshold choice move prevalence. Genuinely new negatives move both. Label which lever you pulled.

The order to look in

Roughly the order of the sections above, because each one can invalidate the ones after it.

  1. Confirm you have the least-processed version available.
  2. Establish the three counts: rows, episodes, units.
  3. Verify every clock and every sampling rate from the files themselves.
  4. Build the missingness picture, including the informative-missingness test.
  5. Write the attrition table, then test whether rule order matters.
  6. Run the event-triggered average before building any features. It can end the project cheaply.
  7. Check comparison-group balance per unit, not in aggregate.
  8. Probe for unit fingerprints in the features.
  9. Choose the split level and state what it protects against.
  10. Record every decision above, with the alternative you rejected and why.

Step ten is the one that converts a careful analysis into a defensible one. The registry below is what it looks like written down.
